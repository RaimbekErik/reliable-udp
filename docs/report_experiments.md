> Draft for the technical report. All numbers come from

> logs in `results/`). Figures are in `results/plots/`. Formulas and their
> derivations are in `docs/derivations.md`.

## 1. Impairment emulator design

The emulator (`emulator.c`) is a single-threaded event loop (`select()` with a
timeout equal to the next scheduled event) with two UDP sockets: one bound to
the port the sender addresses, one on an ephemeral port that faces the
receiver. Because the receiver answers to the source address of the datagrams
it gets, its ACKs arrive on the second socket. That gives two independent
directions, each with its own link rate, queue and propagation delay
(full-duplex).

**Link model.** For each direction the emulator keeps the time at which the
link finishes its current backlog. A packet that arrives at time t starts
transmission at max(t, link free), takes L·8/R seconds (L = datagram size =
12-byte header + payload) and leaves the link at tx_end. The queue holds every
packet whose tx_end is still in the future; if it already holds `queue_pkts`
packets, the new one is tail-dropped. The packet is then delivered at
tx_end + delay + jitter. To keep jitter from reordering packets, a packet is
never delivered before the previous packet of its direction. The computed
transmission start, end and delivery time are written in the `ENQ` log line,
so the analysis knows exactly when the link was busy. Measured scheduling error
of the actual deliveries: median 0.13–0.16 ms, 99th percentile < 0.26 ms
(column `info=late_us` of `DELIVER`), compared with T_f = 8.288 ms.

**Impairments** are applied in a fixed order: scenario actions → loss → bit
corruption → duplication (copy queued right behind the original) → reordering
(held until the next packet of that direction has been queued) → link →
propagation. Lost packets are removed before the link, so they do not use link
time.

**Reproducibility.** A random decision is not drawn from a sequential PRNG,
whose state would depend on how many packets the *other* direction had seen.
Each decision is the hash splitmix64(seed, direction, arrival index, kind of
decision). The n-th packet of a direction therefore always gets the same fate
for a given seed. Two runs with the same scenario (10 % loss + 5 % corruption +
5 % reordering + 5 ms jitter) produced identical impairment decisions (76 of 76
events). A side effect visible in Exp 2: SW and SR runs with the same seed lose
exactly the same packets (both send one packet per sequence number in order),
so their loss realisations are identical. The generator is unbiased:
P(u < 0.05) = 0.0497–0.0502 over 10⁶ draws for seeds 1–6.

**Scenario files** (`key value` lines plus per-packet actions such as
`drop data seq 12` or `delay ack seq 30 ms 200`) are documented in the README;
every run's scenario file is stored next to its logs.

## 2. Methodology

* Test file: `experiments/input.bin`, 262 144 B of pseudo-random bytes (seed
  337), N = 256 packets of 1024 B. Same file in every run.
* All runs use the baseline (1 Mbit/s, 20 ms, queue 128, k = 7, W = 16, payload
  1024) except for the parameters an experiment varies.
* `experiments/run_experiments.py` runs every configuration (202 runs in total)
  sequentially on 127.0.0.1. For each run it stores the scenario, the three logs,
  the command lines and the SHA-256 of the original and the received file.
* Random impairments are repeated over several seeds: 10 seeds per loss rate in
  Exp 2 and 3 seeds per configuration in Exp 4. A 256-packet transfer sees only
  ≈ 13 losses at 5 %, so a single run varies by about ±3.5 losses.
* Metric definitions: see the top of `experiments/analyze.py` (completion time,
  goodput, retransmission overhead in wire bytes, link utilization of the
  forward link counting first transmissions only, SHA-256 integrity, RTT/RTO
  series).

## 3. Experiment 1 — ideal network

| | W | T measured | T theory | goodput meas. | U meas. | U theory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SW | 1 | 12.581 s | 12.386 s | 166.7 kbit/s | 0.169 | 0.171 |
| GBN / SR | 2 | 6.287 / 6.281 s | 6.201 s | 334 kbit/s | 0.337 | 0.342 |
| GBN / SR | 4 | 3.172 / 3.166 s | 3.121 s | 661 kbit/s | 0.669 | 0.680 |
| GBN / SR | 8, 16 | 2.162 s | 2.162 s | 969.9 kbit/s | 0.981 | 0.981 |
| GBN | 32 | 2.162 s | 2.162 s | 969.8 kbit/s | 0.981 | 0.981 |
| SR | 32 | 2.370 s | 2.162 s | 885.0 kbit/s | 0.895 | 0.981 |

(Figure: `exp1_window.png`.) Throughput grows linearly with W while
W < W* = RTT₀/T_f = 5.84 and is flat (link-limited) from W = 8 on, exactly as
derived. In the window-limited cases the measurement is 0.7–1.5 % slower than
theory. That is about 0.7 ms per round trip of processing and scheduling time
in the three programs, which the model ignores (it shows up equally in SW,
GBN W=1 and SR W=1). In the link-limited cases the match is exact to the
millisecond because the link, which the emulator models precisely, is the
bottleneck. GBN and SR behave identically, since neither retransmits anything
on an ideal network.

**SR with W = 32 is the exception (25 spurious retransmissions).** With W far
above W*, the excess W − W* ≈ 26 packets wait in the emulator queue, and by
Little's law the RTT grows to about W·T_f = 265 ms. During the first window the
RTT samples ramp from 48 ms to that value faster than the estimator (gain ⅛)
follows, while the RTO is still at its 200 ms floor. SR starts a per-packet
timer at the moment a packet is *sent*, so packets that wait in the queue for
longer than the RTO time out although nothing was lost. GBN keeps one timer
that is restarted on every ACK (RFC 6298 §5.3), so it is not affected. This is
a property of SR's per-packet timers combined with self-inflicted queueing,
not of the network.

## 4. Experiment 2 — data packet loss (10 seeds per point)

| protocol | p | p̂ realised | T meas. | T theory(p̂) | overhead meas. | overhead theory(p̂) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SW | 5 % | 4.2 % | 15.00 s | 14.77 s | 0.042 | 0.042 |
| SW | 10 % | 8.8 % | 18.41 s | 17.98 s | 0.088 | 0.088 |
| GBN | 5 % | 4.3 % | 5.17 s | 4.63 s | 0.407 | 0.416 |
| GBN | 10 % | 9.3 % | 9.00 s | 8.45 s | 0.593 | 0.615 |
| SR | 5 % | 4.2 % | 3.59 s | 3.47 s | 0.042 | 0.042 |
| SR | 10 % | 8.8 % | 4.67 s | 5.10 s | 0.088 | 0.088 |

(Figure: `exp2_loss.png`; theory at the nominal p is in `tables.md`.) Every
received file is intact. The theory is evaluated at the loss rate p̂ the
emulator actually realised in each run, because 10 seeds of 256 packets still
leave a few tenths of a percent of sampling error.

* **Overhead.** SW and SR resend exactly the lost packets: measured overhead =
  p̂ to three decimals. GBN resends the whole outstanding window per loss. The
  measured 41 % / 59 % matches the Stallings expression (1−p+pW)/(1−p) within
  1–2 points. It is slightly lower because the window is not always full when
  the timer fires (e.g. at the end of the file).
* **Time.** SW: within 1.6–2.4 %, and the remaining gap is the same ~0.7 ms per
  packet as in Exp 1. SR: the stall model (idle time RTO + RTT₀ − W·T_f ≈ 116 ms
  per loss) is accurate at 5 % (−3 %) and overestimates at 10 % (+9 %). At that
  rate two losses often fall into the same stall and share one wait, while the
  model charges each loss separately. GBN: within 10 %. The model charges one
  RTO per loss; the measurement is longer because a loss inside a retransmitted
  window costs another timeout, and the measured RTO is up to 213 ms (RTT samples
  include queueing).
* **Why the costs differ.** In bytes GBN ≫ SR = SW (GBN resends ~W packets per
  loss). In time SW ≫ GBN > SR. SW idles a full RTO per loss and pays RTT₀ per
  packet anyway. GBN restarts its stream an RTO later. SR keeps the link busy for
  W·T_f of the RTO + RTT₀ wait and resends one packet.

## 5. Experiment 3 — propagation delay (jitter = 10 % of delay)

| | D [ms] | RTT₀ | RTT meas. (mean) | U meas. | U theory | timeouts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SW | 20 / 50 / 100 / 200 | 48 / 108 / 208 / 408 | 49 / 109 / 209 / 410 | 0.169 / 0.076 / 0.040 / 0.020 | 0.171 / 0.076 / 0.040 / 0.020 | 0 / 0 / 2 / 2 |
| SR W=16 | 20 / 50 / 100 / 200 | | 131 / 135 / 219 / 426 | 0.981 / 0.954 / 0.587 / 0.303 | 0.981 / 0.955 / 0.613 / 0.319 | 0 / 0 / 2 / 1 |
| SR W=64 | 20 / 50 / 100 / 200 | | 572 / 557 / 553 / 568 | 0.801 / 0.821 / 0.798 / 0.840 | 0.981 / 0.955 / 0.914 / 0.841 | 59 / 43 / 55 / 1 |

(Figures: `exp3_utilization.png`, `exp3_rtt_rto.png`.)

* **Utilization** follows U = W·T_f/RTT₀ (capped by the link). SW collapses from
  0.17 to 0.02. SR W=16 stays link-limited while W ≥ RTT₀/T_f (6 and 14
  packets needed at 20 and 50 ms) and becomes window-limited at 100 ms (26
  needed) and 200 ms (50 needed). Measured values are within 0.002 of theory
  for SW and within 4 % for SR W=16. W=64 is above the requirement at every
  delay, so theory predicts link-limited operation.
* **RTO tracking.** For SW the RTO sits on the 200 ms floor at 20 and 50 ms,
  where SRTT + 4·RTTVAR would be below it. At 100 and 200 ms it follows the RTT
  at a margin of SRTT + 4·RTTVAR ≈ RTT + 20…50 ms. This margin grows with the
  jitter (±10 / ±20 ms per direction). Two spurious timeouts each at 100 and
  200 ms (spikes in the figure) come from single samples beyond the RTO, i.e.
  the jitter tail. The RTO doubles once and returns to normal on the next ACK.
* **SR W=64 at 20–100 ms reaches only ≈ 0.80** instead of 0.91–0.98, with 43–59
  timeouts. This is the Exp-1 W=32 effect, amplified. 64 packets in flight on a
  path that holds only 6–26 packets means the emulator queue holds 38–58 packets,
  and RTT rises to W·T_f + 2D ≈ 570 ms (measured 553–572 ms, Little's law). In
  the figure the RTT samples climb linearly during the first window, the RTO lags
  behind, packets time out while still queued, and each timeout doubles the RTO
  (the vertical spikes). The retransmitted copies take link time away from new
  data. At 200 ms the pipe itself holds 50 packets, the queue stays small, and
  the measurement matches theory (0.840 vs 0.841, 1 timeout). **Lesson:** a
  window larger than the bandwidth-delay product brings no throughput, only
  queueing delay, and with send-time-based timers it causes spurious
  retransmissions.
* **Go-Back-N (not run).** Without loss and with order-preserving jitter, GBN
  would send exactly the same packets as SR and reach the same utilization for
  the same W. On a timeout, however, it resends the whole window: up to 64
  packets at W = 64, plus an RTO of idle time (≥ 0.4 s at 200 ms). GBN restarts
  its single timer on each ACK, so it would not suffer SR's queue-induced
  spurious timeouts. But every real or spurious timeout would cost it ~W times
  more than SR. Large delay-bandwidth products therefore make GBN increasingly
  expensive under any loss.

## 6. Experiment 4 — corruption, reordering, duplication, ACK loss (5 %, 3 seeds)

All 36 received files are identical to the original (SHA-256).

| impairment | SW: T / overhead / timeouts | GBN: T / overhead / timeouts | SR: T / overhead / timeouts |
|---|---|---|---|
| corruption | 19.97 s / 0.107 / 30.7 | 6.08 s / 0.464 / 14.0 | 5.17 s / 0.107 / 30.7 |
| reordering | 16.98 s / 0.078 / 21.7 | 5.18 s / 0.406 / 11.7 | 2.16 s / 0 / 0 |
| duplication | 12.55 s / 0 / 0 | 2.27 s / 0 / 0 | 2.27 s / 0 / 0 |
| ACK loss | 15.10 s / 0.045 / 12.0 | 2.16 s / 0 / 0 | 3.25 s / 0.045 / 12.0 |

(Figure: `exp4_impairments.png`.)

* **Corruption** hits both directions. A corrupted packet fails the CRC-32 and
  is discarded, so it is equivalent to a loss of DATA *or* ACK. That is why SW
  and SR retransmit ≈ 10 % (5 % + 5 %), twice the rate of Exp 2's pure data
  loss. GBN pays a window per corrupted DATA packet. A corrupted ACK is mostly
  covered by the next cumulative ACK.
* **Reordering.** SR buffers the out-of-order packet and ACKs it individually:
  no cost at all (T = 2.162 s, the ideal value). The GBN receiver discards
  everything after the "missing" packet, which arrives one packet late, so the
  sender eventually times out and resends a window: 41 % overhead. SW is
  special: a held packet is released only when the *next* packet of its
  direction arrives. In SW that is the retransmission after a timeout, so every
  reordering event costs one RTO (21.7 timeouts ≈ 5 % of ≈ 2·265 packets).
* **Duplication** is harmless for all three: the receivers recognise the copy
  (RX_DUP) and re-ACK it, and the senders ignore the duplicate ACK. The only
  cost is the link time of the copies (2.27 s vs 2.16 s ≈ +5 %).
* **ACK loss.** GBN's cumulative ACK makes a lost ACK irrelevant: the next ACK
  acknowledges the same packets (0 retransmissions, ideal time). SR and SW ACK
  each packet individually, so every lost ACK means a timeout and a needless
  retransmission (overhead 4.5 %).

## 7. Experiment 5 — sequence-number limits

Impairment: every ACK of the first window is dropped (`drop ack index 0-(W-1)`).
This is the worst case of both proofs in `docs/derivations.md`: the receiver
has the whole window, the sender none of the ACKs, so the sender times out and
resends the entire first window.

| protocol | k | W tested | intact for | SHA-256 differs for | derived limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GBN | 3 | 1–8 | W ≤ 7 | W = 8 | 2^k − 1 = 7 |
| GBN | 4 | 1–16 | W ≤ 15 | W = 16 | 15 |
| SR | 3 | 1–5 | W ≤ 4 | W = 5 | 2^(k−1) = 4 |
| SR | 4 | 1–9 | W ≤ 8 | W = 9 | 8 |

(Figure: `exp5_limits.png`.) All 38 runs agree with the derivation. The drops
are scripted, so each failure is reproduced on every run. Mechanism of the
GBN k=3, W=8 failure: the receiver accepts packets 0–7 and now expects packet 8,
whose number is 8 mod 8 = 0. The sender, having no ACK, resends packet 0 with
number 0, and the receiver writes the old payload in place of packet 8. SR
k=3, W=5: after packets 0–4 the receiver window is packets 5–9, numbers
{5, 6, 7, 0, 1}. The retransmitted packet 0 falls into it and is buffered as
packet 8. In both cases the transfer "completes" without any error being
detected, and only the end-to-end hash reveals the corruption.

## 8. Limitations of the emulator and the measurements

* Loss is applied before the link (lost packets use no link time). Corrupted,
  duplicated and reordered packets do use it.
* A reordered packet is released after `max_hold_ms` (1 s) if no packet
  follows. This only matters at the very end of a transfer. While a packet is
  held, the next one is not itself held.
* Timing relies on `select()` on a shared machine: deliveries are 0.13–0.16 ms
  late on average. The three programs add ~0.7 ms per round trip, so
  window-limited runs are up to 1.5 % slower than theory.
* The queue length counts the packet in transmission. Queue overflow never
  occurred in the experiments (W ≤ 64 < 128).
* Loss rates are realised over only ~260 packets per run, hence the 10 seeds
  and the comparison at the realised p̂.
